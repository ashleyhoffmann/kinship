Body care

Bodies are always repairing themselves. Body care is mostly about giving that repair what it needs. We start with the mouth.

1.1

Why teeth decay

Hundreds of kinds of bacteria live in every mouth, and most of them help. Trouble starts when conditions favor the few that make acid, mainly Streptococcus mutans. Plaque is a community, and changing the conditions changes who lives there.

Acid and dryness are the problem

Acid pulls minerals out of enamel. Saliva neutralizes acid, so a dry mouth (from mouth-breathing, medicines or not drinking enough water) leaves teeth unprotected. After each sip or snack, saliva takes about 30 minutes to bring the mouth back to neutral. How often you eat or drink matters more than how much.

Teeth need minerals

Enamel is mostly calcium and phosphate. In the quiet times between meals, saliva puts minerals back, and fluoride or hydroxyapatite toothpaste helps the surface rebuild harder. A cavity is where losing has outpaced rebuilding for a long time. Before that, early damage can reverse.

Sugar feeds the bacteria

Sugar doesn’t harm teeth by itself. Acid-making bacteria eat it, along with starches that linger (crackers, chips, dried fruit), and give off acid. Some drinks are acidic on their own, sugar or not, like soda (diet too), citrus, kombucha, sports drinks and lemon water.

What helps

  • Have acidic or sweet drinks with a meal rather than sipping them for hours.
  • End a meal on something neutral: water, nuts, crunchy vegetables or xylitol.
  • After something acidic, swish with water and wait 30–60 minutes before brushing.
  • Brush twice a day, along the gumline, with fluoride or nano-hydroxyapatite paste. Spit without rinsing, especially at night.

1.2

Xylitol and mouthwash

Xylitol

Acid-making bacteria take in xylitol as if it were sugar, but can’t use it, so they grow more slowly and make less acid. Its sweet taste also gets saliva flowing. Dr. Ellie Phillips suggests 6–10 g a day, in 3–5 small doses after eating. It works through steady daily use.

Make your own

Simple and free of dyes and alcohol.

Rinse powder

  • ½ cup baking soda
  • ¼ cup xylitol
  • 15–20 drops peppermint oil
  • 5–8 drops clove oil

Mix the oils into the dry powder until no damp spots are left, and store it in a glass jar. Stir ¾ tsp into ¼–½ cup of warm water, swish, and spit. The baking soda neutralizes acid. It keeps for months, and the scent fades after 2–3 months.

Xylitol mints

  • ½ cup xylitol (about 100 mints)
  • 2–3 drops peppermint oil
  • 1 drop clove oil, at most

Melt the xylitol on low heat until clear. Cool it briefly, stir in the oils, and drop ¼ tsp amounts into tiny silicone molds. Let them set at room temperature, then store airtight. Each mint is about 1 g.

Mix essential oils into the dry powder first, because a drop of pure oil can burn your gums. Tea tree, cinnamon and wintergreen are best left out.

Store-bought

Dr. Ellie Phillips uses Closys (chlorine dioxide), original Listerine (essential oils) and ACT (0.05% sodium fluoride). Anything with the same active ingredient does the same job. Check labels for dyes such as Blue 1, alcohol and sweeteners. A fluoride rinse or fluoride toothpaste adds the mineral help that a homemade rinse doesn’t.

Xylitol is very toxic to dogs, even in small amounts. Keep jars, gum and mints out of reach.
A dentist can catch and treat what home care can’t, like deep cavities, cracked teeth, infections, gum disease and pain. Dental school clinics and community health centers often cost less. In California, Medi-Cal Dental covers many people. Call 211 to find care nearby.
