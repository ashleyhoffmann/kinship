Fun fact

Kids around the Bay hear “poison ivy” on TV, but the plant on local trails is its cousin, poison oak. Same oil, same rash, same rule: leaves of three, let it be. It can be a shrub, a vine up a tree, or bare sticks in winter that still carry the oil.

Unit 1

Learn the land

Who lives around the Bay

Every plant and animal here is meeting their own needs: food, water, shelter, a safe place to raise young. Most of the time, ours and theirs fit together easily. This unit is about the few who need extra room, what the house needs to stay sound, and the many who make the land a joy to know.

1.1

How to look

Identifying is noticing a few features at once, never just one. A name is a guess until three or four features agree.

  • Shape and size: tall or low, a vine or a shrub, a body as long as a finger or a forearm.
  • Leaves: their shape, whether they sit in pairs or alternate up the stem, and whether they come in threes.
  • Smell: crush one leaf (not of a plant from the Hands off list). Fennel smells of licorice, bay of strong spice, yerba buena of sweet mint.
  • Where and when: sun or shade, wet or dry, which month. Miner’s lettuce in a shady spot in March is a different guess from a green clump on a hot August roadside.

Every card in this unit carries one of five words, so you know what kind of meeting it is:

Trail rules for small hikers

  1. Leaves of three, let it beBare sticks in winter too.
  2. Ask before you tasteEvery plant, every berry, every time. Mushrooms: never.
  3. Look before you reachHands and feet only where you can see: under logs, in rock piles and woodpiles is where snakes and spiders rest.
  4. Middle of the trailTicks wait on grass tips and poison oak lines the edges.
  5. Watch from far awayA baby animal alone is usually waiting for a parent who’s nearby.
  6. Never touch a batOr any wild animal acting tame, sleepy or sick. Tell a grown-up.
  7. Big cat? Stand tallDon’t run. Arms up, loud voice, stay with your grown-up.
  8. Check when you’re homeA tick check (hair, ears, waist, knees) and wash hands.

Children learn the land by walking it next to adults who stop, crouch and smell. Name one plant per walk and let them find it again on the way back. By the end of a season they’ll be pointing out poison oak to you.

Install a free app like Seek by iNaturalist (no account needed) and use it as a second opinion together. An app can suggest a name; it can’t tell you something is safe to eat.

Give space: animals who need room

None of these animals is out to get anyone. Each one protects themselves or their young when they feel cornered. Room, noise and watching from a distance meet everyone’s need for safety.

1.3

Hands off: plants and fungi to leave be

Plants can’t run, so many make strong chemicals to avoid being eaten. These are the ones around the Bay where a touch, a taste or even the smoke can hurt. Look as much as you like.

Poisons: Plants and companions (houseplants, lilies and cats)

1.4

Protect the house: visitors the house is feeding

A house is wood, water and shelter, which is exactly what some small neighbors are looking for. In the forest, termites and wood fungi recycle fallen trees into soil; they don’t know the difference between a log and a beam. A few can quietly cost a home thousands of dollars, so knowing the early signs is real care for the house. The strategy is always the same: notice early, and stop offering wood, water, food and a way in.

When a home is sold, a Wood Destroying Pests and Organisms report is standard. Renters can ask to see the last one, and can report leaks and rot to the landlord in writing.

1.5

Local wonders

The Bay is one of the richest places on Earth for life: fog-fed redwoods, oak hills, salt marsh and open ocean, all within a bus ride. These are some of the neighbors who make it home.

This is Ohlone land, and the lands of the Coast Miwok, Bay Miwok, Patwin and other Native peoples, who have tended these oaks, grasses and creeks for thousands of years and still do. Many of the plants in this unit were food and medicine long before they had English names.

1.6

The forager’s eye: herbs and wild foods

Learning to spot rosemary hedges, a patch of miner’s lettuce or a bay tree changes every walk into a treasure hunt. Most of the time it’s about knowing, smelling and saying hello, and only sometimes about bringing something home.

  1. Sure, or leave itThree or four features agree, and someone who knows has checked it with you the first time.
  2. Ask firstYour yard, a friend’s with permission, a community garden. Most Bay Area parks don’t allow picking; check before you gather.
  3. Clean spotsAway from busy roads and old painted walls, above dog height, and not where anything was sprayed.
  4. Take a littleA few leaves from many plants. Leave most for the birds, the bees and next year.

White sage is sacred to many California Native nations and is overharvested from the wild. Garden sage and rosemary from a hedge meet the same kitchen and scent needs.

1.7

Twins: who looks like whom

Most bad days with wild plants and animals start with a look-alike. Learn the tell for each pair, and when in doubt, treat it as the one that needs more care.

Wild fennel or poison hemlock?

ForageWild fennelThread-thin feathery leaves, yellow umbrella flowers, a strong licorice smell from every part.
Hands offPoison hemlockFlat lacy leaves like parsley, white umbrella flowers, smooth stems with purple blotches, a musty smell. Deadly.

Smell and stem: no licorice or a blotched stem means leave it. They grow side by side on the same roadsides.

Wild carrot or poison hemlock?

WonderWild carrot (Queen Anne’s lace)Hairy stems, often one tiny dark purple flower in the middle of the white umbrella.
Hands offPoison hemlockSmooth, hairless stems with purple blotches; usually much taller.

Leave the whole white-umbrella family alone until an expert walks you through it in person.

Bay leaf or oleander?

ForageCalifornia bay laurelA tree; a crushed leaf smells sharply of spice, strong enough to make your eyes water.
Hands offOleanderA shrub with showy flowers; long leathery leaves with little or no smell. A heart poison.

Crush and sniff. No strong spice, no cooking.

Wild onion or death camas?

ForageWild onionGrass-like leaves that smell like onion when crushed.
Hands offFremont’s death camasGrows in the same grassy hills with a similar bulb and creamy star flowers, and no onion smell. Toxic.

No onion smell, no onion.

Chestnut or buckeye?

ForageChestnutSeveral nuts packed inside a very spiny husk; a little pointed tassel on each nut. Mostly from stores and planted trees here.
Hands offCalifornia buckeyeOne big shiny brown nut in a smooth, leathery husk. Toxic raw. Ohlone cooks made it food by long leaching; don’t try at home.

Spiny husk with a pointed nut, or leave it.

Puffball or death cap “egg”?

WonderPuffballRound and white; cut top to bottom it’s smooth solid white all the way through.
Hands offYoung death capAlso an egg shape; cut open it shows the outline of a cap and stem inside.

Leave all wild mushrooms to experts who can check them in person.

Honeybee or yellowjacket?

WonderHoneybeeFuzzy, golden-brown, busy on flowers, rarely interested in you.
Give spaceYellowjacketSmooth, shiny, bright yellow and black, zigzagging around drinks and meat.

Fuzzy and on flowers is a bee. Shiny and at your sandwich is a yellowjacket.

Black widow or false widow?

Give spaceWestern black widowGlossy jet black, round belly, red hourglass underneath.
WonderFalse widowBrown to plum-purple, pale markings on top, no red hourglass. Common in Bay Area houses; a bite is mild.

Look underneath (from a distance): red hourglass means give her space.

Gopher snake or rattlesnake?

WonderGopher snakeSlim head barely wider than the neck, a tail that tapers to a point. May hiss and shake their tail in dry leaves to bluff.
Give spaceNorthern Pacific rattlesnakeA wide, triangular head on a thin neck, a heavy body, a rattle (sometimes missing).

You don’t need to be sure: give every snake a few long steps of room and walk around.

1.8

If something happens

Calm, quick calls make the biggest difference. Save these in your phone before the next hike.

911Trouble breathing, a snake bite, swelling of the face or throat, someone faint or confused.
Poison Control · 1-800-222-1222Free, 24 hours, in many languages. Call for any plant, mushroom, berry or spider bite, even if they seem fine. Bring or photograph what was eaten.
Animals · your vet or ASPCA Animal Poison Control, (888) 426-4435For a dog or cat who ate a mushroom, a plant or was bitten. The ASPCA line may charge a fee.
Wild animal in troubleLocal animal services or a wildlife rescue. For a seal, sea lion or whale on the beach: The Marine Mammal Center.

For a snake bite: no cutting, sucking, ice or tight bands. Keep still, take off rings and watches, and get to help.
