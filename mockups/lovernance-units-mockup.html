Unit 1

Lovernance

“To change something, build a new model that makes the existing model obsolete.” Credited to Buckminster Fuller.

I’m calling my envisioned alternatives lovernance: what I want the law to allow and encourage now. As our shared thinking catches up, communities can hold it themselves and government can step back. Every situation is case-by-case, but here are three generalized kinds of land, each set up through zoning.

All are built on kinship; nobody charges their sister rent. What we give flows from relationship and need, and care reaches outward (especially once our needs feel met), from our household to loved ones, neighbors, and strangers, including animals, plants, and land.

All start in the same place: the minimum isn’t what rent costs, it’s what it costs to meet needs directly. Rain caught on a tarp, seeds from a seed library and cuttings from a neighbor cost close to nothing (Water: Sources). The one thing that takes money is land, so that’s where governance comes in.

1.1

Needs-meeting commons

Public

One fallback that isn’t a punishment: a good place to live where your needs are met.

Anyone can walk in, especially people with nowhere else to go. Most commons won’t be built from scratch; they’ll be conversions. The house next door becomes a commons, and the whole block gains a station for getting needs met. Want a different life? You’re free to try your luck elsewhere.

How needs get met

  • Day one: a bed, water and food. Rest comes first, and nobody is asked to work at the door.
  • A station for the block: water refills, showers, toilets, laundry, a pantry, thrift, repair and a tool library, open to neighbors.
  • Food for residents first, then outward to neighbors.
  • Work that meets real needs. Worker co-ops are like unions that own the shop, and existing unions are friends: a plumber meets real needs too. The direction is “I’m on call in the neighborhood to fix your pipe, and my family has a home,” not price-gouging for executives.
  • Worker co-ops make what people need: beds, natural-fiber clothing, tools, solar, internet and seed.
  • One price for everyone. When there isn’t enough, need comes first and residents get a set discount.
  • One at a time: a bed, not 75 beds to resell. Trade in an old model toward a new one, and the old one gets repaired or passed on.
  • Not a tragedy. Commons last when the people who use them make the rules, with small responses first and conflicts worked through close to home (Elinor Ostrom; Dialogue).
  • The land is held by a community land trust. Members give time instead of money. Rentals can become co-ops that residents own, at real cost.
  • Anyone can leave, with their skills and credit for what they gave.

California today

  • Helps City of Santa Barbara v. Adamson (1980): the state right to privacy struck down a cap on unrelated people sharing a house.
  • Helps Assessors must count a land trust’s resale limits, keeping property tax lower (§402.1).
  • Helps The Williamson Act (1965) taxes contracted farmland as a farm. The Rainwater Capture Act (2012) lets you use roof rain without a water right.
  • Helps The Worker Cooperative Act (2015), plus SB 1079 (2020) and San Francisco’s COPA (2019), which give tenants, land trusts and co-ops a chance to buy when certain homes sell.
  • Helps In a declared shelter crisis, a city or county can swap state building and zoning rules for simpler local ones, but only for shelters on public land (Gov. Code §8698.4, until 2036).
  • County by county Class K lets people build simpler rural homes, only where adopted. Shelter and safe-parking rules vary too.
  • In the way Rural zoning, contractor-grade building codes, land prices, and, since Grants Pass (2024), citations for sleeping outside.
1.2

Collective-owned homes

Owned together

Homes given to the program, held together and shared by match.

Dense or spread out. People who live at the commons have collective-owned homes too, just high-density ones. A single suburban house given in is a low-density one. Whoever lives there joins give-and-get: the backyard might hold a vegetable bed or the block’s water filter, meeting needs for the neighborhood and for them. Ready to move? Give your whole home, and get a comparable one. That’s how land moves from private control into shared hands: one home at a time, by choice.

How needs get met

  • Give a home, get a kin credit for a comparable home anywhere in California. Or keep living in yours for life, and it goes to the program after.
  • Inherit a house you don’t want? Trade it. Once homes aren’t tied to jobs, Humboldt’s forests are open too.
  • Rank homes honestly. The match is built so honesty is your best move, and nobody ends up with a home they like less than the one they gave. Homes people want most simply get more requests (top trading cycles, the idea behind kidney exchange).
  • Children first. Families with kids get first pick of private homes, and the match keeps kids in their school district.
  • Matched like college housing: for how long, who must live with you, and whether you’re open to housemates.

Homes owned by companies

  • Companies are people underneath, often pension funds for teachers and firefighters. Their needs are steady income and a clean way out.
  • Sell to the program at an appraised price paid over years: steady income, with the tax spread out too.
  • Renters get first chance to buy their homes as co-ops, and property managers can stay on as paid stewards.
  • Sell for less, and the difference counts as a gift.

California today

  • Federal The ROAD to Housing Act (2026): from January 7, 2027, for-profit owners of 350 or more single-family homes can’t buy more, with exceptions like build-to-rent. It doesn’t force sales, so a way out matters.
  • Federal McKinney-Vento: students without stable housing can stay in their school of origin, with transportation.
  • Federal Selling over time spreads the tax across years (installment sales, §453). Giving your home while keeping the right to live in it for life can count as a charitable gift (§170).
  • In the way A home trade is a change in ownership, which resets property tax to today’s value.
  • Not yet law Zoning for given homes, kin credits for comparable homes, and a statewide match. Doors for advocacy (see Stairs for change below).
1.3

Give-and-get program

Private

Your home stays yours. Opt in, and your land gets lighter rules for meeting needs.

Your land is your slice of heaven for as long as you live there and look after it, and it can pass to whoever you choose. Opting in rezones it into the program. It’s invite-only: you decide who stays, like a close friend in a tough spot. Open it to people you don’t know, after a meeting where either of you can say no, and you get the most support of all. Give in ongoing gifts or one-time gifts; every gift is a pledge, not a contract.

Ways to give

  • Grow. Gardeners grow food with you. You keep enough for your household, and the abundance goes to the commons. Start with one bed, and ask for watering help anytime.
  • Water and make. Room for rain tanks, a filter station or a maker’s bench, with first use for you.
  • Care. Dog walks, babysitting swaps, help for someone with a disability, meals after a birth or a loss.
  • Shelter. Someone you invite sleeps in their car, a tent or a cabin. They owe you nothing. Host someone you don’t know, and you get a support worker, a mediator and the home-care fund behind you.
  • Bridge homes. “My kids want it in five years.” Someone lives there until then, knowing it ends, with a commons home guaranteed next. Families with children go first.
  • Lend it to friends. Away every Christmas? Let a friend’s family stay. Nobody tracks it; a friend’s home instead of a hotel is already the gift.
  • Never-empty pledge. Your home won’t sit unused for more than a year. Nobody takes it from you.

Shelter without a $500k ADU

  • A guest needs a safe place to sleep and a bathroom. A car, tent or small cabin works with bathroom access (yours, a commons station nearby, or a serviced portable or certified composting toilet), smoke and CO alarms, a fire extinguisher and a clear way out.

Safety for givers

  • A state-backed home-care fund repairs damage. Damage means someone is struggling, so it’s met with cleaning help and care, not fines.
  • One check-in a year on the house’s needs, by a neutral town steward or by you. Neighbors can flag the land, not the people.
  • You choose how much the home can change: keep it as is, decorate and restore, or make it yours. Gardens, ramps and rain tanks stay.

Safer together

  • Disaster means a home tonight. If a give-and-get home burns or floods, the commons houses you right away while co-op builders rebuild. That promise is the real safety, more than any check.
  • Insurance owned by the people it covers. A members’ mutual for give-and-get and commons homes: no shareholders, and any surplus goes back into fire-hardening, not profit.
  • Harden the whole block together. Neighbors, gardeners and time gifts do the work (ember-resistant vents, a clear 5-foot zone, irrigated gardens as green buffers), which earns home and community discounts.
  • Automatic payouts. A neighborhood policy pays the local fund as soon as a fire or flood is measured, with no claims or adjusters.
  • A way out of the riskiest places. Home trades let people move somewhere safer without losing what they gave.

Thanks, not rewards

  • Basics are never earned, and everyday care isn’t counted. What gets written down is coordination, not credit (Alfie Kohn).
  • Safety without hoarding. When health care, food and a home are reliably shared, hoarding has less and less to do.

California today

  • In the way Prop 13 (1978) caps property tax at 1% of the last sale price, so the longer someone holds, the less they pay, even for an empty home.
  • In the way Private insurers have pulled back, and the state’s last-resort FAIR Plan grew to about 697,000 policies by June 2026, up 157% since 2022. The Sustainable Insurance Strategy lets insurers raise rates if they write more in high-risk areas; whether that’s happening is disputed.
  • Helps “Safer from Wildfires” (2022) requires insurers to give discounts for home and community fire-hardening, such as Firewise communities.
  • Helps Fremont (2024) became the first U.S. city to buy its own parametric flood policy, which pays automatically once flooding is measured. The California Earthquake Authority (1996) shows a publicly managed statewide pool can work.
  • In the way Building codes treat anywhere people sleep as a home, and most places require a flush toilet. The shelter-crisis shortcut covers only public land.
  • In the way The Ellis Act stops cities from forcing owners to keep renting (and in August 2026 an appeals court tentatively said it preempts San Francisco’s empty-homes tax), so pledges stay voluntary.
  • In the way Anything a guest gives for a stay can look like rent, making them a tenant with rules a car or tent can’t meet.
  • Helps Owner move-in is a just cause to end a tenancy (SB 567): family moves in within 90 days and stays a year. That fits bridge homes.
  • Helps Owners who let people garden are shielded from most injury suits (Civil Code §846), unless they get something in return. Food donors are liable only for gross negligence (AB 1219).
  • Helps Some counties already pay landlords for damage when they house people leaving homelessness (Marin: up to $3,500). In-Home Supportive Services pays caregivers, family included.
  • Not yet law A give-and-get zone with emergency-housing safety rules (like Appendix X of the state residential code) on private land, a hosted-shelter category, a home-care fund in every town, a members’ mutual for give-and-get homes, and a statewide wildfire pool like the Earthquake Authority.
