Use: how much, how clean

Ability: Can work out how much water a household needs, and how clean each use has to be

Most US homes use about 80 gallons per person a day, indoors and out. Humanitarian groups plan around 4 gallons (15 liters) per person for drinking, cooking and basic washing, and 1 gallon is the short-term minimum to stay alive. Most of what we use doesn't need to be drinking-clean.

Four grades of water (and what gray water is)
Grade 1Drinking-cleanDrinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula, ice, washing produce eaten raw, animals' bowls.
Grade 2Clean, boil if unsureWashing hands and bodies, dishes, laundry, watering veggies. Rain or stored water fits here.
Grade 3Gray waterUsed once: shower, bath, bathroom sink, laundry. Good for flushing and for the soil around non-edible plants.
Grade 4Black waterToilet waste (and in many places, the kitchen sink). Goes to sewer, septic or a proper compost toilet.
  • Use gray water within a day. Stored gray water starts to smell and grow germs.
  • Send it into soil or mulch, not onto leaves you eat or root crops.
  • Plant-friendly soaps (low salt, no boron or bleach) keep the soil happy.
  • A bowl in the sink while washing veggies catches grade 2 water for the houseplants.
Special cases
Add to the drinking water
Nursingabout 3–4 extra cups a day
Pregnantabout 1–2 extra cups a day
  • Breastfed babies get their water from milk; the nursing parent drinks more instead.
  • Formula needs grade 1 water. When you're unsure of it, boil and let it cool.
  • Illness (fever, vomiting, diarrhea) raises needs fast. Oral rehydration salts help: 6 level tsp sugar + ½ tsp salt in 1 liter of clean water.
  • Elders often feel thirst later than they need water. A full glass within reach helps.
  • Medical equipment: CPAP humidifiers use distilled water; home dialysis uses a lot. Ask the care team how much to keep on hand.
  • Cloth diapers, reusable pads, incontinence: add a laundry load or a soak bucket per week.
  • Animals drink more in heat and while nursing; keep bowls in shade and topped up.

Keeping it good

Food-grade only, never anything that held chemicals. Keep it dark and cool: algae only need light, water and a few nutrients, so a clear jug on a sunny porch can turn green in weeks. Date it: refresh water you bottled yourself every 6 months. Spread it out, so one damaged spot doesn't take it all, and away from gasoline, paint and cleaners, which pass through plastic. Pour, don't dip.
